I am a 5th-year Ph.D. candidate in Political Economics at the Stanford Graduate School of Business.

My research examines media capture and political accountability using quantitative methods, including causal inference and text analysis. Most of my work focuses on Mexico, where I build original datasets from government advertising contracts, news archives, and legislative records.

Working Papers

Allocating Influence: Government Advertising in Mexico’s News Market (Job Market Paper)

[Abstract] [Paper]

In many countries, governments are major media advertisers. Due to their prominent role, it is unclear whether they use their market power for political ends, and, if so, how they leverage that power. I study these questions in Mexico, where the government is one of the largest advertisers. Using novel data on advertising contracts and a comprehensive corpus of articles from national print media, I provide evidence of political favoritism. I use the 2018 transition from a right-wing to a left-wing federal administration and show that aligned outlets are 18.3 percentage points more likely to receive a contract. I benchmark these allocations against what a government would choose if it were buying audience reach efficiently or targeting its own voters. The observed allocation overpays outlets based on their ideology. The allocation of advertising to aligned media allows them to expand their newsroom capacity and their news production relative to misaligned outlets. The increase in production attracts more readers and raises circulation at aligned outlets. Lastly, I show that the resulting circulation gains are driven mostly by the expansion in content rather than by shifts in reader preferences. Overall, these findings reveal a mechanism of media capture in competitive markets that requires neither censorship nor an exchange of coverage for revenue.

Party Discipline across Electoral Mandates and Career Concerns: Theory and Evidence from Votes and Speech in the Mexican Congress

with Gabriel López-Moctezuma

[Abstract]

We study how party discipline varies with electoral mandates and career concerns. In our model, partisan benefits induce discipline insofar as leaders rally enough support among their legislators to make their promises credible. Absent reelection, constituency concerns offset the leader’s promises, leaving district legislators less disciplined than their list copartisans. Reelection makes these benefits electorally valuable, directly improving district legislators’ local valence and list legislators’ renomination prospects, and indirectly strengthening the leader’s credibility in both tracks. We test the empirical implications of the model in Mexico’s mixed-member Congress around the reform introducing consecutive reelection. We measure disagreement with party leaders in votes and speech across seven Congresses (2006–2024): disagreement falls in both tracks after the reform, most among district incumbents. Leaders, in turn, redirect partisan benefits toward district incumbents ex ante and reward the disciplined while punishing dissenters ex post. Accountability reforms that leave ballot access, list placement, and campaign finance in party hands can strengthen the organization they were meant to check.

The More the Merrier? The Effects of Council Size on Representation and Local Governance

[Abstract] [Paper]

Does broader political representation improve local governance? I use population thresholds in Mexican state law that fix the size of a municipal council, comparing municipalities just above a threshold with those just below, and link novel data on the characteristics of every council member to the output that council produces in office. Crossing a threshold seats 12 percent more representatives, and they are negatively selected: councils gain more members with a basic level of education and with no prior political experience, while parity rules hold the share of women fixed. Governance does not improve. Larger councils approve about half as many initiatives, a third fewer per member, across a narrower agenda, and their alignment with the preferences of citizens decreases; spending is unchanged in level and composition. I interpret this as negative selection at the margin of the party list: the supply of candidates is smooth at the threshold, but lists are closed and ranked, and parties concentrate their strongest candidates where the return to quality is highest.
